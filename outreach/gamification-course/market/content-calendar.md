## Post 1 - Points and badges (the myth)

Most gamification advice stops at points and badges. Stars on a chart, a leaderboard,
a badge for finishing. It works for about a week, and then the class sees through it.

Real engagement comes from something quieter. A clear goal you can picture. Fast
feedback so you know if it is working. A challenge that is the right size, not too easy
and not crushing. A little choice in how you get there. And a reason to care that is
bigger than the reward.

I spent years learning this the hard way in front of real classes, and I put all of it
into a course for teachers. If you want the short version, I wrote a free one page guide
with the six things that actually pull a room in. It is in the first comment.

## Post 2 - The kid who checks out

There is a child in almost every class who checks out early. Not because they cannot,
but because the lesson was never built with them in mind. For twenty years, most of them
in special education, those are the kids I have cared about most.

Here is what I learned. If you design a lesson so the child who struggles can still take
part, with an easier path, a non verbal way to succeed, a quieter option, you do not
just help that one child. The whole class gets a better lesson. Inclusion is not extra
work bolted on. It is simply better design.

That idea sits at the heart of everything I teach now, including a full course on
game based learning that works for every learner. More in the first comment.

## Post 3 - AI gave me my evening back

I used to spend evenings making a worksheet, then a differentiated version, then a
version for the two kids who needed something else again. Now I do it in about fifteen
minutes with a little AI, and I spend the saved hour on the actual teaching.

The trick is not to let AI think for you. It is to let it do the slow, fiddly part so
you can do the human part better. Plan faster, differentiate without dread, and still
teach your students to use it honestly rather than pretending it does not exist.

## Post 6 - Let your students build a game

Ask a class to play a game and you get a fun afternoon. Ask them to build one and you get
coding, storytelling, design, teamwork and a room that will not go home.

You do not need a computer lab full of specialists. With Delightex, formerly CoSpaces,
students build 3D worlds, add simple block code, and step inside them in AR or VR. I have
watched kids who never say a word in class become the ones explaining their world to
everyone else.
